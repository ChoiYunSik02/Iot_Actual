# Abstract

A rotating semi-active tuned liquid column damper (RS-TLCD) tunes its frequency, turntable angle and orifice to control a bi-directionally eccentric 30-storey building, but the original control law identifies the frequency only after the building has responded. We re-implement the 92-DOF building of the original study with the literal rotation law and ask whether the damper frequency and angle can be set in advance, and whether a neural network that predicts them from the first 1-5 s of ground motion improves on a constant setting. Across eleven records of seven earthquakes, a constant setting chosen without the earthquake under test was ahead of the original logic in direction (+0.84 ± 1.24 to +1.31 ± 1.20 points of mean six-index reduction at the event level), but the difference is not resolved with seven events and disappears when the constant may use a wider tuning range than the device. A network trained on 2,000 synthetic and real-variant samples did not improve on the constant setting in nine conditions under leave-one-earthquake-out evaluation, and the dominant frequency of the input was unrelated to the best damper frequency. A switch during the event has 3.65 points of post-hoc headroom that none of sixteen causal rules realised. The results delimit what the present model and data can show.

**Keywords:** tuned liquid column damper; semi-active control; torsional coupling; neural network; early ground-motion information; leave-one-earthquake-out


# 1. Introduction

Tall buildings with eccentric mass and stiffness respond to an earthquake in both horizontal directions and in torsion at the same time. A passive tuned liquid column damper (TLCD) acts along one axis and loses effectiveness when the building frequency or the direction of the response differs from the design assumption. Fu et al. (2025) proposed a rotating semi-active TLCD (RS-TLCD) for a 30-storey bi-directionally eccentric building: a turntable rotates the liquid column towards the direction of the response, a pump changes the liquid height and thus the damper frequency within ±5% of the building frequency, and an orifice changes the damping. In their control law the damper frequency and the angle are chosen from the response of the roof, using a wavelet transform of the displacement measured after the building has started to move, and the pump and the turntable need further time to reach the target. The damper can therefore only follow motion that has already happened.

Machine learning has been applied to semi-active control in several ways. Wang et al. (2022) trained an ensemble of long short-term memory networks online to drive the orifice of a tuned liquid wall damper without prior training, Kim and Kim (2023) used a deep Q-network to command the voltage of a magnetorheological damper in a mid-story isolation system, and AlHamaydeh et al. (2024) predicted the next 1–200 samples of the ground acceleration from the preceding 0.5 s window for the real-time control of intelligent damping devices. Farsijani et al. (2025) used random forests to relate ground-motion features to the performance of tuned mass dampers and found that the tuning ratio is the most influential variable, and that the sensitivity to detuning grows with the impulsiveness of the motion. These studies either adjust the actuator command from the measured response or predict the waveform itself; to our knowledge none of them sets the frequency and the angle of a rotating liquid damper before the structure responds.

This study asks whether the damper can be set in advance from the early part of the ground motion, which is the situation of a building far from the epicentre, where information on the incoming motion may be available before the strong shaking arrives. We keep the building, the damper and the damping (orifice) law of Fu et al. (2025) unchanged, and replace only the way in which the damper frequency and the turntable angle are chosen. The study makes four contributions.

1. A re-implementation of the 92-degree-of-freedom model in which the rotation law is taken literally from the original equations; we show that the equations as printed couple the damper frequency and the mass projection, and that this reading changes the semi-active result (Sections 2 and 3).
2. A decomposition of what can be gained by setting the damper in advance: a constant pre-set value, the best constant for each earthquake (Oracle), and settings that change once during the event (Section 4).
3. A neural network that predicts the damper frequency and angle from the first 1–5 s of the ground motion, trained on 2,000 synthetic and real-variant samples and evaluated with leave-one-earthquake-out cross-validation on eleven records of seven earthquakes, together with a test of the alternative of tracking the dominant frequency of the input (Section 5).
4. A statement of what the present model, data and evaluation protocol do and do not show, with event-level uncertainty (Sections 4–6).

Section 2 describes the building and the damper, Section 3 the ground motions and the validation of the model, Section 4 the reference strategies and the headroom, Section 5 the prediction network, and Section 6 the conclusions.


# 2. Target building and RS-TLCD model


## 2.1 Bi-directionally eccentric 30-storey building

The benchmark structure is the 30-storey bi-directionally eccentric frame of Fu et al. (2025), used here without modification so that the proposed controller can be compared with the original one on identical ground. Each floor is a rigid diaphragm with two horizontal translations and one rotation. The floor mass and mass moment of inertia are m = 9.216 × 10⁵ kg and J = 2.797 × 10⁸ kg·m² at every level. The lateral stiffness is 1.021 × 10⁹ N/m for the first storey and 1.6213 × 10⁹ N/m for the others in both directions, and the torsional stiffness is 3.1651 × 10¹¹ and 5.0261 × 10¹¹ N·m/rad, respectively. The centre of mass is offset from the centre of stiffness by e_x = 6 m and e_y = 2 m. Inherent damping is Rayleigh damping with a 2% ratio in the first two modes.

With the damper degrees of freedom appended, the equation of motion is

$$
\mathbf{M}\ddot{\mathbf{U}} + \mathbf{C}(t)\dot{\mathbf{U}} + \mathbf{K}(t)\mathbf{U} = -\mathbf{M}\mathbf{E}\ddot{\mathbf{U}}_g \qquad (1)
$$

where **U** = [u_x1 … u_x30, u_dx, u_y1 … u_y30, u_dy, u_θ1 … u_θ30]ᵀ has 92 entries, **Ü**_g = [ü_xg, ü_yg]ᵀ is the bi-directional ground acceleration, and **E** assigns ü_xg to all X degrees of freedom (floors and damper) and ü_yg to all Y degrees of freedom. The translation–torsion coupling is **K**_xθ = e_y **K**_x and **K**_yθ = e_x **K**_y, built from the storey stiffness only (the damper rows are not coupled to rotation).

The uncontrolled model reproduces the ten X-direction natural frequencies of the original study (0.3373, 1.0112, 1.6831 … Hz) to four decimal places. Because of eccentricity, the fundamental X mode couples with the first Y and torsional modes into three modes at 0.2714, 0.3373 and 0.3953 Hz.

## 2.2 Rotating semi-active TLCD

The RS-TLCD is a U-shaped liquid column mounted on a turntable on the roof. Its natural frequency depends only on the effective liquid length L:

$$
\omega_d = \sqrt{2g / L}, \qquad L = 2H_v + \lambda B \qquad (2)
$$

so that pumping water between reservoirs changes the vertical water height H_v and therefore ω_d. Following the original design, the tunable range is ±5% of the fundamental frequency, [0.3204, 0.3542] Hz (L = 4.83–3.96 m), and a frequency change takes Δt_f = 2.6 s. The participating liquid mass is m_d = 694,767 kg (1.5 times the passive TLCD mass, 3% of the first modal mass) and the area ratio is λ = 0.237.

The head loss of the horizontal section with an orifice of opening ratio κ is

$$
\delta(\kappa) = (1-\lambda)^2 + 0.5|1-\lambda| + \lambda^2\left[(1-\kappa) + 0.707(1-\kappa)^{0.375}\right]^2 \kappa^{-2} \qquad (3)
$$

and enters the equivalent linearised damper as

$$
c_d(t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho\,\frac{A_v^2}{A_h}\,\delta(\kappa)\,|\dot u_d - \dot u_{n}| \qquad (4)
$$

The turntable rotates the column axis by θ(t), so the equivalent liquid mass in the two horizontal directions is m_dx = m_d cos θ and m_dy = m_d sin θ, and the stiffness is k_dx = m_dx ω_d² cos θ and k_dy = m_dy ω_d² sin θ (Eq. 5, Section 3.2). The damper frequency projected on each axis is therefore ω_d√(cos θ) and ω_d√(sin θ), and the directional masses sum to m_d(cos θ + sin θ), up to √2 m_d. The rotation speed is 30°/s.

## 2.3 Original control law (wavelet identification)

In the original RS-TLCD the three actuators are driven by feedback from the roof response:

1. **Frequency (every 2.6 s).** The instantaneous frequency of the roof displacement in each direction is identified by a Morlet wavelet transform, clipped to the tunable range, and combined by displacement weighting, ω_d = (|D_y|ω_dy + |D_x|ω_dx)/(|D_x| + |D_y|) [Eqs. 10–11]. The pump then moves the water level over the next 2.6 s.
2. **Damping (every 1 s).** The orifice switches between κ = 0.8, 0.4 and 0.1 according to the signs of the relative velocity and the floor acceleration in the two directions [Eq. 12].
3. **Angle (every 2.6 s).** The turntable is driven towards θ = arctan(D_y,max / D_x,max) over the last t_R seconds [Eq. 13].

Because the frequency is identified *after* the building responds and the pump needs another 2.6 s, the device can only react to motion that has already occurred. The present study keeps all three actuators and the damping law (item 2) unchanged and replaces only how the target frequency and angle (items 1 and 3) are chosen.

## 2.4 Numerical solution and modelling assumptions

Equation (1) is integrated with the Newmark average-acceleration method at Δt = 0.01 s; **M**, **C** and **K** are reassembled at every step because m_dx, m_dy, k_dx, k_dy and c_d vary in time. The wavelet transform is implemented directly (a causal half-Morlet kernel using only past samples, 0.1–2 Hz, 80 scales) so that it is realisable on-line; no toolbox routine is used.

The original paper leaves some quantities unspecified. The following choices were made and are applied identically to every controller compared in this study:

- **Cross-sections.** A_v is obtained from m_d = ρA_vL at the nominal length, and A_h = A_v/λ.
- **Damping velocity.** Eq. (4) uses the damper velocity relative to the roof, consistent with the mass–spring–dashpot form of the damper.
- **Damping calibration.** With the sections above, Eq. (4) gives an equivalent damping ratio of only 0.05–0.5%, and the passive TLCD amplifies the response for some records. Eq. (4) is therefore multiplied by a constant factor of 30, chosen so that the mean reduction of the passive TLCD over the six indices matches the original Table 7 (15.56% vs 16.00%; X-RMS 33.19% vs 33.54%). The κ-dependence of δ (a factor of 0.7–10) is retained, so the semi-active damping law remains active (Section 3).
- **Rotation in stiffness.** The original expression is applied literally (cos θ twice in k_dx). Applying it once, or conserving the damper mass (cos² θ, sin² θ), are treated as sensitivity cases in Section 3.2.
- **Angle window.** t_R = 2.6 s, equal to the angle update interval.
- **Damping levels.** c_min, c_opt and c_max correspond to κ = 0.8, 0.4 and 0.1.


# 3. Ground motions and model validation


## 3.1 Ground motions

The original study used fifteen bi-directional PEER records: twelve stations of the 1979 Imperial Valley earthquake, Pacoima Dam (1994 Northridge), KJMA (1995 Kobe) and CHY006 (1999 Chi-Chi). Eight of these pairs could be obtained in their original PEER format (Nos. 1, 2, 3, 4, 11, 13, 14 and 15 of the original Table 2); for the remaining seven only one component or aftershock records were available, and they were excluded rather than substituted. Three additional pairs are used to widen the frequency content: El Centro 1940 (N–S/E–W components, from a public compilation; whether it is identical to the corrected PEER version was not checked) and two moderate Korean events recorded at long epicentral distance by broadband velocity sensors of the KMA network (Pohang 2017, ML 5.4, station KS.BUS2, 98 km; Gyeongju 2016, ML 5.8, station KS.CHJ2, 164 km). The Korean records have peak accelerations of only 1–3 cm/s², were converted from velocity by the instrument sensitivity, differentiated and band-passed at 0.1–8 Hz, and are used for 60 s starting 5 s before the P arrival. They are far-field records and serve only as waveforms with different spectral content once scaled. All eleven pairs are used in the evaluation of Sections 4 and 5; the eight PEER pairs alone are used for the comparison with the original results below.

The eleven pairs belong to only seven independent earthquakes: five of them (Nos. 1, 2, 3, 4 and 11) are stations of the same Imperial Valley event. This matters for the statistics: in Sections 4 and 5 records of the same earthquake are always treated as one group, both when training and when averaging differences between methods.

Following the original study, each horizontal component is scaled to a peak ground acceleration of 35 cm/s² (frequent earthquake level of the Chinese code). The original paper does not state whether scaling was applied per component or per pair; per-component scaling is adopted here.

## 3.2 Rotation law of the RS-TLCD

Equations (16), (17) and (20) of the original study give the equivalent damper mass in the two directions as m_dx = m_d cos θ and m_dy = m_d sin θ, and the damper stiffness in the X direction as

$$
k_{dx}(t) = \frac{2\,m_{dx}\,g}{H_{vL}+H_{vR}+\lambda B}\cos\theta(t) = m_{dx}\,\omega_d^2\cos\theta(t) \qquad (5)
$$

so that the stiffness carries cos θ twice (once through m_dx), and the frequency of the damper projected on the X axis is ω_d√(cos θ), not ω_d. The first version of the present model (Draft v0) used k_dx = m_dx ω_d², i.e. cos θ only once. Table 3 shows that the reading matters for the semi-active result, and the literal Eq. (5) is adopted from this draft on. Passive and S-TLCD cases do not rotate and are unaffected.

**Sensitivity of the RS-TLCD reproduction to the rotation law** (eight PEER pairs, mean reduction of the six indices, damping calibration of Section 2.4):

| Rotation law | Passive TLCD | S-TLCD | RS-TLCD | RS − passive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| k = m_dx ω_d² (v0) | 15.56 | 15.43 | 15.31 | −0.25 |
| **Eq. (5), literal (adopted)** | 15.56 | 15.43 | **16.12** | **+0.56** |
| mass-conserving, m_dx = m_d cos²θ | 15.56 | 15.43 | 12.89 | −2.68 |
| *Original study (15 pairs)* | *16.00* | *16.09* | *18.80* | *+2.80* |

Only the literal reading moves the semi-active result towards the original, in the direction of the reported advantage of rotation. Note that with m_dx = m_d cos θ and m_dy = m_d sin θ the two directional masses add up to m_d(cos θ + sin θ), up to √2 m_d at 45°, so the equations as printed do not conserve the liquid mass. The mass-conserving projection reproduces the original RS-TLCD result considerably worse (12.89%), which suggests that the printed formulation, not a mass-conserving one, underlies the reported performance. We retain the printed formulation so that comparisons with the original controller are made on identical equations, and we state this property as a limitation: absolute reductions at θ ≠ 0 should be read as those of the printed model.

## 3.3 Validation

**Natural frequencies.** The ten X-direction natural frequencies of the uncontrolled model coincide with the original Table 1 to four decimal places, and the first-mode mass participation is 83.76%, as reported.

**Limitation.** The semi-active cases are reproduced less well. In the original study the RS-TLCD improves on the passive TLCD by 2.8 points on average; in the present model the improvement is 0.56 points (16.12% vs 15.56%), and the RS-TLCD mean is 2.7 points below the original, mainly through the peak and torsional indices (θ peak 6.69% vs 11.50%, θ RMS 10.68% vs 16.03%). Plausible causes are the unreported cross-sections (which set the absolute damping level), details of the wavelet identification, the rotation law discussed above, and the seven missing records. Because every controller in this study — including the original wavelet law used as the baseline — is evaluated in the same model and with the same records, the discrepancy is expected to affect absolute values more than the ranking of methods, but this cannot be guaranteed: a model error can affect different controllers differently. The conclusions of Sections 4 and 5 are therefore stated for the present model.


# 4. How much can be gained by setting the damper in advance?


## 4.1 Reference strategies

All strategies use the original damping law (orifice switching, 1 s) and differ only in how the damper frequency f and the turntable angle θ are chosen. The mean reduction of the six indices of Section 3 is the performance measure, averaged over the eleven records.

| Label | Strategy | Needs the earthquake? |
|---|---|---|
| E1b | passive TLCD detuned to 0.3204 Hz (no rotation) | no |
| E2 | original RS-TLCD, wavelet identification after the building responds | no |
| Fixed | RS-TLCD set from t = 0 to one (f, θ) cell, the same for all earthquakes; the cell with the highest mean label score of the training data (Section 5.2), excluding the earthquake under test | no |
| Oracle | for each record the best of 49 cells (7 frequencies in the tunable range × 7 angles, 0–90°), set from t = 0 | yes — not realisable |

The Oracle is the upper bound of *constant* settings only; Section 4.4 treats settings that change once during the event.

Two findings follow.

1. **A constant (f, θ) set before the earthquake is ahead of identifying the frequency afterwards, in direction.** The Fixed cell of Table 4 is chosen without the earthquake under test (highest mean label score of the training data), so it is deployable; the record-averaged Fixed − E2 is 1.5–2.2 points, but averaged over the seven earthquakes it is +1.31 ± 1.20 (nominal, 5 of 7 earthquakes), +1.71 ± 1.52 (0.3204 Hz, 3 of 7) and +1.00 ± 0.67 (0.3542 Hz, 5 of 7). If the cell is instead chosen from the real records of the other earthquakes, the nominal building gives +0.84 ± 1.24 points (5 of 7); the sign is the same under the other rotation laws (+1.52 ± 0.77, +2.20 ± 1.46) and damping scalings (+0.74 ± 1.05 at ×20, +0.68 ± 1.18 at ×40), but for the adopted model the difference is within one standard error. If the fixed setting may use a ±20% range while the original device keeps ±5%, the difference is −0.39 ± 0.70. In the first 8 s after the trigger the fixed setting reduces the peak roof displacement by 5.10% against 3.92% for the original logic, consistent with the original logic acting only after the building has responded.
2. **Knowing the earthquake would add a further 1.2–3.3 points** (Oracle − Fixed), which is the maximum that a predictor choosing one constant per earthquake could gain. It is largest when the building is softer than designed (3.3 points at 0.3204 Hz).

## 4.3 Where the headroom is

For the nominal building and the fixed cell chosen on the eleven records themselves (a choice favourable to the fixed strategy), the best constant is (0.3542 Hz, 45°) with 15.83%, and the Oracle gives 17.60%, i.e. 1.77 points of headroom:

- **Frequency alone** (θ fixed at its best value): 0.73 points. **Angle alone** (f fixed): 0.81 points. In the printed model the two are coupled, because the damper frequency projected on each axis is ω_d√(cos θ) (Section 3.2); the best fixed frequency is the upper bound of the tunable range.
- The mean reduction depends strongly on the angle: 7.6, 11.4, 15.3, 15.8, 10.2, 6.7, 3.5% at θ = 0, 15, 30, 45, 60, 75, 90° (f = 0.3542 Hz). The Oracle angle is 30° for eight records and 45° for three; the Oracle frequency is the upper bound for seven records.
- The headroom is unevenly distributed. The loss of the best fixed cell relative to the Oracle is 7.4 points for Pohang 2017, 2.5 (El Centro Array #11), 2.1 (El Centro 1940), 2.0 (KJMA), 2.0 (El Centro Array #3), 1.3 (Pacoima Dam), 1.0 (Brawley), 0.8 (CHY006), 0.4 (Gyeongju) and 0 for the Mexicali and Differential Array stations. A single record (Pohang 2017) accounts for a large part of the mean headroom.

**Caveat.** At θ = 45° the printed model gives each direction 0.71 m_d of liquid mass and a frequency of ≈ 0.84 ω_d, so the best constant detunes the damper below the building frequency. This is a property of Eq. (5) as printed (Section 3.2), not a design recommendation.

## 4.4 Settings that change once during the event

A constant setting is not the only way to use advance knowledge: a damper kept at its standby state (0.3373 Hz, 0°) during the strong motion and switched afterwards may perform differently. We evaluated a single switch from the standby state to a cell (f, θ) at a time t_s, and searched the 49 cells for each switching rule.

*Switch at a fixed delay after the trigger (0.5 gal).* With t_s = trigger + 8 s the best constant cell reaches 17.18% (vs 15.83% for a constant from t = 0), and the per-record maximum over cell and delay is 19.48%, i.e. 3.65 points above the best constant. For the same cell the gain is concentrated in three Imperial Valley stations and Pacoima Dam (Differential Array +9.2, Mexicali +5.8, Pacoima +2.8, Brawley +1.7 points at 8 s), while El Centro Array #11 (−0.5), KJMA (−6.0), El Centro 1940 (−0.4) and Gyeongju (−1.8) lose and Pohang is unchanged.

*Is this realisable and does it generalise?* Strong motion starts 2–25 s after the trigger in these records, so a delay after the trigger is not a physical reference. We therefore tested causal rules that use only the past ground acceleration: a delay after the strong-motion onset (peak so far ≥ 0.1 m/s², about 30% of the scaled peak), the time since the running peak was last exceeded, and the decay of the 2-s mean-square acceleration relative to its running maximum. The cell was chosen on the training earthquakes only and evaluated on the held-out one (leave-one-earthquake-out; seven earthquakes, Imperial Valley records treated as one).

| best rule chosen by cross-validation among 16 | — | −0.89 ± 1.06 | 2/7 |

No rule improves on the constant setting beyond the uncertainty. In-sample differences of 1.4–2.3 points per record come mostly from records of one earthquake (Imperial Valley, five of eleven records) and Pacoima Dam; when that earthquake is left out, the constant cell chosen on the remaining earthquakes also performs poorly on it (12.0% on the Differential Array, against 22.4% for the best cell), so the record-level comparison overstates the differences. Switching too late is harmful (−1.2 points at trigger + 16 s, −3.6 at + 24 s). **We therefore regard the time-varying headroom as an upper bound that we could not realise with a causal rule, and we do not claim it.**

## 4.5 Summary and limits of this chapter

- A constant pre-set damper is ahead of the original post-hoc identification in direction (1.5–2.2 points averaged over records; +1.31 ± 1.20 and +0.84 ± 1.24 over earthquakes for the nominal building, with two ways of choosing the constant), but the difference is not resolved at the event level.
- Perfect knowledge of the earthquake would add 1.2–3.3 points if one constant per earthquake is chosen; the headroom is concentrated in a few records.
- These values are for the present model (Section 3.3): the RS-TLCD reproduction is 2.7 points below the original, and the Oracle is evaluated on eleven records from seven earthquakes. Differences below about 1 point between strategies cannot be resolved.
- The fixed strategy and the Oracle use the 35 cm/s² scaling of the original study; the ±20% tunable range was recomputed with the literal rotation law (Section 4.2) and removes the advantage of the fixed setting over the original device.


# 5. Predicting the damper setting from the early ground motion


## 5.1 Problem

The learning task is to choose the constant damper setting (f, θ) from the first T_obs seconds of the ground motion after the trigger, so that the reduction of Section 3 is maximised. This is stated as the prediction of the setting that would have performed best, not of the frequency of the incoming motion: the two coincide only if the best setting is determined by the frequency content of the earthquake, which Section 4.3 shows to be only partly the case (the best constant is the upper bound of the tunable range, and the angle matters as much as the frequency).

## 5.2 Training data and labels

A sample consists of a bi-directional ground motion and a building stiffness multiplier k ∈ [0.81, 1.21] (fundamental frequency ±10%). Each sample is labelled with the 7 × 7 map of mean reductions obtained by simulating all 49 constant settings of Section 4.1 (49 simulations per sample); the network learns the map minus its sample mean, so that a poor prediction of the best cell still tends to choose a good neighbouring cell.

- 1,489 synthetic ground motions: Kanai–Tajimi spectrum with Clough–Penzien high-pass filtering applied to white noise, ground frequency 0.5–8 Hz (log-uniform), ground damping 0.2–0.6, duration 15–40 s, Jennings-type envelope, X–Y correlation 0–0.6, intensity ratio 0.5–1.
- 511 variants of the real records: horizontal rotation by 0–180° and time scaling by 0.7–1.4 (which shifts the dominant frequency).
- All samples are scaled to 35 cm/s² per component, with 10 s of free vibration appended.

## 5.3 Input and model

Input (69 values), computed from the first T_obs seconds after the trigger (0.5 gal): log Fourier amplitude of each horizontal component in 32 geometric bands between 0.1 and 5 Hz, normalised to the maximum (64); the logarithm of the peak acceleration of X and of the Y/X peak ratio; sin 2φ and cos 2φ of the principal axis of the two components; and the relative change of the building's fundamental frequency (assumed known from ambient vibration). Model: a fully connected network 69–64–64–(49), ReLU, trained with AdamW (learning rate 2 × 10⁻³, weight decay 10⁻³), mean-square loss on the label map, 400 epochs, the epoch with the lowest loss on a 1/7 validation split is kept. No hyper-parameter search was made.

## 5.4 Evaluation protocol

- **Leave-one-earthquake-out.** For each test record, all records of the same earthquake and all variants derived from them are removed from the training set (the five Imperial Valley stations are one group). Eleven networks are trained per seed.
- **Constant baseline.** The constant cell is the one with the highest mean label score on the same training set, so it is as deployable as the network.
- **Five training seeds**; seed variability is reported separately from the variability across earthquakes.
- **Two ways of applying the prediction.** *Preset*: the predicted cell is applied from t = 0, as if early-warning or other prior information supplied the first T_obs seconds; this is an idealisation. *Online*: the damper stays at its standby state (0.3373 Hz, 0°) and the command is issued at trigger + T_obs, with the pump (2.6 s) and the turntable (30°/s) dynamics of the original design.
- T_obs = 1, 3, 5 s; building 0.3204, 0.3373, 0.3542 Hz.
- Differences between methods are averaged over the seven earthquakes and reported as mean ± standard error across earthquakes.

Further observations:

- **The network does not recover headroom beyond the constant.** The constant setting alone recovers 40–56% of the gap between E2 and the Oracle (1.5–2.2 of 2.7–5.5 points, record average; see Section 4.2 for the event-level uncertainty); the preset networks recover 33–56% of the same gap. Expressing the result as "share of the Oracle gain recovered" relative to the original method would credit the network with a gain that a constant also provides.
- **Observation time has no systematic effect.** The preset results at 1, 3 and 5 s differ by at most 0.4 points for a given building (e.g. 15.21, 15.58 and 15.55% for the nominal building), comparable to the seed standard deviation (0.1–0.7), and they do not increase monotonically with T_obs. Averaged over records the online and preset variants are within 0.6 points of each other, but averaged over earthquakes the online variant is below the constant setting in all nine conditions (−0.16 to −1.08 points). The frequency of the Oracle cell is predicted with an error of 6–15 mHz, i.e. 18–43% of the 34 mHz tunable range, for all T_obs.

This is the delayed-switch effect of Section 4.4, which does not generalise across earthquakes.
- **Korean records.** For the softer building the network exceeds the constant by 1.4–2.0 points on Pohang and Gyeongju (preset, mean of the two records), and is within ±0.3 points for the other two building states. This is two records and cannot be distinguished from variability across seeds and records; Pohang also has the largest headroom (Section 4.3).

## 5.6 Discussion

The result is that, with the information of 1–5 s of ground motion, the network did not select a setting better than the constant one. We see three reasons, none of which we claim to be proven.

1. **Resolution.** The spectral resolution of a window of 1–5 s (0.2–1 Hz) is coarser than the whole tunable range (0.034 Hz) and than the building frequency itself, so the frequency content of the window cannot discriminate settings inside the range. The extra frequency points used in the input FFT interpolate the spectrum and add no information.
2. **The headroom depends on what is hard to predict.** In the previous version of the model, giving the network the magnitude, distance and site class of 1,000 simulated earthquakes (Boore 2003 stochastic method) recovered none of the Oracle headroom in five-fold validation; the headroom there arose from phase and stress-drop variability that the early-warning parameters do not contain. We have not repeated this experiment with the present model.
3. **Little headroom per record.** Apart from a few records (Pohang 2017), the loss of a good constant relative to the Oracle is 0–2.5 points, of the same size as the variability between seeds, so a network trained on 2,000 synthetic samples and evaluated on eleven records cannot show a significant gain.

These findings are specific to the architecture, the data and the evaluation of this study (a small network on synthetic and rotated real records, no hyper-parameter search, no regret-based or recurrent model, eleven test records from seven earthquakes). They show that a gain from prediction is not demonstrated here, not that it cannot exist.

## 5.7 Reading the task as tracking the frequency of the incoming motion

An alternative formulation, suggested by the aim of tuning the damper to the incoming vibration, is to predict the dominant frequency of the ground motion and to set the damper frequency to it. In the present model this reading is not supported by the data. The damper can only move within ±5% of the building frequency (0.3204–0.3542 Hz), whereas the dominant frequencies of the eleven records lie between about 0.2 and 4.1 Hz (Table 2 lists the per-component values), so a predicted input frequency would almost always be clipped to one end of the range. The dominant frequency and the energy near the building frequency are unrelated to the best damper frequency. The centroid has a negative sign, i.e. records with more high-frequency content prefer a lower damper frequency, which is the opposite of tracking the input frequency; with seven independent earthquakes it does not reach significance (|ρ| = 0.63 < 0.79). Seven of the eleven records have their best frequency at the upper end of the range (0.3542 Hz), which reflects the coupling of f and θ in Eq. (5) (Section 4.3) rather than the input spectrum. We therefore keep the formulation of Section 5.1, in which the network chooses the damper frequency and angle that would have performed best; the orifice (valve) law is not predicted and remains the original one (Section 2.3).

## 5.8 Limitations

- Eleven records from seven earthquakes; the five Imperial Valley records are not independent, and the Korean records are only two.
- The labels depend on the literal rotation law of Eq. (5), which does not conserve the damper mass (Section 3.2); the RS-TLCD reproduction is 2.7 points below the original (Section 3.3).
- The preset application is an idealisation. A realistic early-warning application must also account for transmission, computation and actuation times.
- The building stiffness multiplier is assumed known. Noise on the input, as in the robustness test of the original study, was not examined.
- The tunable range of the original design (±5%) was used; the ±20% range of the earlier model version has not been recomputed with Eq. (5).

# 6. Conclusions

Using the literal rotation law of Fu et al. (Eq. 20) in a 92-DOF, bi-directionally eccentric 30-storey model, we found:

1. A constant, pre-set damper setting (frequency and angle fixed from t = 0, chosen on the training split) was ahead of the original wavelet-based RS-TLCD logic in mean six-index reduction under the adopted literal model (+0.84 +- 1.24 points when the constant is chosen from the real records of the other earthquakes, +1.31 +- 1.20 when it is chosen from the training labels; event level, better in 5 of 7 events in both), and under rotation laws A (+1.52 +- 0.77) and C (+2.20 +- 1.46), and under damping x20 (+0.74 +- 1.05) and x40 (+0.68 +- 1.18). The sign is consistent, but with seven events the difference is within one standard error for the adopted model, so we call it a tendency, not a demonstrated gain. With a +-20% tuning range for the fixed setting it did not hold (-0.39 +- 0.70). In the first 8 s after the trigger the fixed setting reduced peak roof displacement slightly more than the original logic (5.10 vs 3.92 %), consistent with the original logic acting only after the building responds.
2. Reading the task as tracking the frequency of the incoming motion is not supported: the dominant input frequency was unrelated to the best damper frequency (Spearman ρ = −0.31 over eleven records, −0.20 over seven earthquakes), and the damper can move only ±5% around the building frequency.
3. A learned predictor of the setting from the first seconds of ground motion did not show a reliable gain over the constant setting. Under leave-one-earthquake-out evaluation (seven events), event-level standard errors exceeded the differences. We therefore report that a gain is not demonstrated under this architecture, data and protocol, not that prediction is impossible.
4. A post-hoc, time-varying one-time switch showed headroom of about 3.65 points, but none of the causal switching rules we tested realised it under leave-one-earthquake-out cross-validation.

Limitations: remaining ~2.7-point gap to the original RS-TLCD reproduction; one building model; 11 records of 7 events; damping calibration (c_scale = 30); no sensor noise test.
