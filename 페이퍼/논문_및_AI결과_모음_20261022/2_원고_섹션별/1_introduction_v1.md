# 1. Introduction

Tall buildings with eccentric mass and stiffness respond to an earthquake in both horizontal directions and in torsion at the same time. A passive tuned liquid column damper (TLCD) acts along one axis and loses effectiveness when the building frequency or the direction of the response differs from the design assumption. Fu et al. (2025) proposed a rotating semi-active TLCD (RS-TLCD) for a 30-storey bi-directionally eccentric building: a turntable rotates the liquid column towards the direction of the response, a pump changes the liquid height and thus the damper frequency within ±5% of the building frequency, and an orifice changes the damping. In their control law the damper frequency and the angle are chosen from the response of the roof, using a wavelet transform of the displacement measured after the building has started to move, and the pump and the turntable need further time to reach the target. The damper can therefore only follow motion that has already happened.

Machine learning has been applied to semi-active control in several ways. Wang et al. (2022) trained an ensemble of long short-term memory networks online to drive the orifice of a tuned liquid wall damper without prior training, Kim and Kim (2023) used a deep Q-network to command the voltage of a magnetorheological damper in a mid-story isolation system, and AlHamaydeh et al. (2024) predicted the next 1–200 samples of the ground acceleration from the preceding 0.5 s window for the real-time control of intelligent damping devices. Farsijani et al. (2025) used random forests to relate ground-motion features to the performance of tuned mass dampers and found that the tuning ratio is the most influential variable, and that the sensitivity to detuning grows with the impulsiveness of the motion. These studies either adjust the actuator command from the measured response or predict the waveform itself; to our knowledge none of them sets the frequency and the angle of a rotating liquid damper before the structure responds.

This study asks whether the damper can be set in advance from the early part of the ground motion, which is the situation of a building far from the epicentre, where information on the incoming motion may be available before the strong shaking arrives. We keep the building, the damper and the damping (orifice) law of Fu et al. (2025) unchanged, and replace only the way in which the damper frequency and the turntable angle are chosen. The study makes four contributions.

1. A re-implementation of the 92-degree-of-freedom model in which the rotation law is taken literally from the original equations; we show that the equations as printed couple the damper frequency and the mass projection, and that this reading changes the semi-active result (Sections 2 and 3).
2. A decomposition of what can be gained by setting the damper in advance: a constant pre-set value, the best constant for each earthquake (Oracle), and settings that change once during the event (Section 4).
3. A neural network that predicts the damper frequency and angle from the first 1–5 s of the ground motion, trained on 2,000 synthetic and real-variant samples and evaluated with leave-one-earthquake-out cross-validation on eleven records of seven earthquakes, together with a test of the alternative of tracking the dominant frequency of the input (Section 5).
4. A statement of what the present model, data and evaluation protocol do and do not show, with event-level uncertainty (Sections 4–6).

Section 2 describes the building and the damper, Section 3 the ground motions and the validation of the model, Section 4 the reference strategies and the headroom, Section 5 the prediction network, and Section 6 the conclusions.
