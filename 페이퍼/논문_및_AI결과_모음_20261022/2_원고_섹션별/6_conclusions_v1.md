# 6. Conclusions

This study re-implemented the 92-degree-of-freedom, bi-directionally eccentric 30-storey building and the rotating semi-active TLCD of Fu et al. (2025) with the rotation law as printed (Eq. (5); Eq. (20) of the original study), and asked whether the damper frequency and the turntable angle can be set before the strong motion. A constant setting fixed from t = 0 and chosen without the earthquake under test was ahead of the original wavelet-based logic in the mean reduction of the six indices, by +0.84 ± 1.24 points when the constant was chosen from the real records of the other earthquakes and by +1.31 ± 1.20 points when it was chosen from the training labels, and it was better in five of the seven earthquakes in both cases. The sign was the same under the two alternative rotation laws of Table 3 (+1.52 ± 0.77 and +2.20 ± 1.46 points) and under damping calibration factors of 20 and 40 (+0.74 ± 1.05 and +0.68 ± 1.18 points), and in the first 8 s after the trigger the constant setting reduced the peak roof displacement slightly more than the original logic (5.10% vs 3.92%), consistent with the original logic acting only after the building responds. With seven earthquakes, however, the difference is within one standard error for the adopted model, and it disappeared when the constant setting was allowed a ±20% tuning range (−0.39 ± 0.70 points); it is therefore reported as a tendency rather than a demonstrated gain.

A neural network that predicts the setting from the first 1–5 s of the ground motion did not show a reliable gain over the constant setting under leave-one-earthquake-out evaluation: in all nine conditions the event-level standard errors exceeded the differences. Reading the task as tracking the frequency of the incoming motion is not supported either, because the dominant input frequency was unrelated to the best damper frequency (Spearman ρ = −0.31 over eleven records and −0.20 over seven earthquakes) and the damper can move only ±5% around the building frequency. A single switch of the setting during the event showed a post-hoc headroom of about 3.65 points, but none of the causal switching rules that were tested realised it under the same cross-validation. These results show that a gain from prediction is not demonstrated with the present architecture, data and protocol, not that prediction is impossible.

The main limitations are the remaining gap of about 2.7 points to the original RS-TLCD result, a single building model, eleven records of seven earthquakes, the calibrated damping factor of 30, and the absence of a sensor-noise test.
